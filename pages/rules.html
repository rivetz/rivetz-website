Rules Do Not Change Laws

Follow the SEC's new crypto framework exactly and you will produce a token offering that meets the case-law definition of a security

By Steven Sprague · September 28, 2026

My company sold a utility token in 2017. The SEC sued us in 2021 and we lost in 2024. That is over, we have consented not to argue it further, and I am not asking anyone to reopen it.

I am doing the opposite. I am trying to operate as though that ruling is exactly what it is — good law — and to help other people do the same. Which is how I ended up writing this, because when you actually try it, you discover the new rules do not tell you how.

So this is not about my case, and it is not about anything that happened in 2017. It is for the person sitting down right now to plan a token launch under the new framework, doing the responsible thing, reading the rules carefully, and intending to follow them exactly. That person is taking a risk they have almost certainly not been told about, and it is the largest one in the plan.

A rule is not a law

This distinction sounds academic until it takes your company.

A rule is what an agency says it will do. A law is what a court will hold. An agency can soften its rules, change its chairman, publish an FAQ and mean every word of it — and none of that touches a published judicial holding. When the two disagree, the court wins.

Everyone is reading the new framework as though it changed the law. It did not. It cannot.

What changed the law was the enforcement campaign of 2020 to 2024, and by the only measure that counts, it worked. Case after case, courts accepted that describing what you are building, and the market you intend to serve, is enough to make your sale an investment contract. No promise of returns required. In our case the court pointed at statements that "clearly tied the value of RvT tokens to Rivetz's goal of creating a security ecosystem for mobile devices," and that was the ballgame.

That is not agency policy that moves with an administration. It is precedent. It sits in the reporters and it outlives everyone who wrote it.

The rules now require the evidence

Here is the part that should stop any founder cold.

The proposed rules ask an issuer to file a plan of development, and to describe the crypto asset's "current and anticipated ecosystem." Projections of future performance are encouraged.

Read that against the sentence that lost my case. They are the same sentence.

Compliance manufactures the exhibit.

You are being asked to write down, on a government form, the precise category of statement that four years of litigation established will satisfy the test. And nothing in four hundred pages says that a disclosure you were required to make cannot later be used as proof that you needed to make it.

A founder who does everything right, follows the framework to the letter, and files exactly what is asked of them, ends up holding a well-documented description of an anticipated ecosystem. Which is what a security looked like the last four times a court examined one.

And the SEC is no longer the one who collects

This is the part almost nobody has priced, and it is the reason the first two sections matter.

If you sold an unregistered security, every buyer can demand their money back. Not damages. Their money, returned, on handing back the token. It does not require fraud. It does not require a lie, a broken promise, or proof that anyone lost a penny. It asks one question — was this a security — and if the answer is yes, you write the cheques.

There is no good-faith defence to it. Doing your best does not help.

And notice the shape of it. The buyers who made money keep their gains. The buyers who lost money hand back the token and take their purchase price. It is a one-way option on your treasury, exercised by whoever is underwater. For most projects that is not a lawsuit. It is the end.

And this is not a legacy problem that time cures. The clock on that claim starts when you sell — so it starts the day you launch, and it starts again with every sale after that. For a project raising now and distributing over the following years, the exposure is not behind you. It is the thing you are about to create, continuously, for as long as the offering runs.

The SEC cannot call this off. It is not the SEC's claim. The Commission can decide not to sue you; it cannot decide that on a buyer's behalf. It can publish guidance; a private plaintiff can ignore it, and since Loper Bright a court owes that guidance less deference than it once did.

So here is the trap, stated plainly.

The agency that built the theory has stepped back from using it. The rules it is writing now require you to generate the evidence that theory runs on. And the people who can actually use that evidence against you were never the agency, are not bound by anything it publishes, and have a remedy that does not require them to prove you did anything wrong.

What the new documents say about that

Nothing. I mean that literally, so I checked it rather than asserting it.

I read the new interpretation and the proposed rules end to end — 469 pages — and searched for every way a lawyer might refer to somebody other than the Commission enforcing the securities laws. "Plaintiff" does not appear. Neither does "defendant," "rescission," "class action," "private right of action," "cause of action," or "strict liability." Not once, in either document.

Liability is discussed. It is discussed twice, and both times it is fraud liability — the survivable kind, the kind that turns on whether you said something untrue, the kind a careful operator can defend by being honest. The kind you cannot defend against is not mentioned at all.

The document warns you about the risk you can manage and is silent on the one that ends you.

I do not think that is bad faith. I think the Commission wrote a document about what the Commission will do, and founders need a document about what anyone can do. Those are different documents and only one of them exists.

It also just got cheap to come after you

One more thing changed, and it has nothing to do with crypto.

An aggressive legal theory has always been limited by what it costs to use. Someone had to find it, read the cases, map them onto your facts and draft the complaint. That expense was never a feature of the law — it was a property of the people who practised it, and it quietly protected a lot of companies for a long time.

That cost has collapsed, because AI is now ordinary equipment in a law office. I am not speculating: the analysis behind this piece, which would once have been a week of an associate's time, took me part of a day. I am not a lawyer. I used an AI system to do it, and I am telling you that because it is the whole point.

Point the same tools at the holdings instead of at the rulebook and you do not get a blog post. You get a claim.

The doctrine did not get broader. The number of people who can reach the end of it did.

What I actually conclude

Not that anyone is acting in bad faith. The current direction is a real improvement on what came before, and the people writing it are trying.

But a regulator expanded a statute through litigation, succeeded, and then stepped back — leaving behind an expanded theory it no longer intends to use and cannot retract, in a field where the cost of using it has fallen to almost nothing. Silence is not retraction. An agency cannot retire its own precedent by declining to mention it.

Only two things close this door: Congress, or an appellate court. Until one of them does, a token strategy run in the United States with United States buyers carries a risk that cannot be drafted around. I have tried. I kept every document and took every precaution the field recommends, and I still cannot construct the path. If someone can, I would sincerely like to see it.

I filed all of this with the Commission on September 28, 2026, in the open comment file for Regulation Crypto Assets, along with three specific requests: describe what the case law now holds; state plainly that these rules do not affect private claims and that compelled disclosure is not evidence; and if the exposure cannot be fixed by rulemaking, say so out loud. None of that requires changing a single rule. It only requires telling people the door is open.

Until then, understand what is actually on the table. Not a prohibition — you can launch, and many will. But the honest description of the deal is that you will follow a set of rules that require you to document exactly what a court has repeatedly held makes a token a security, and then carry an unhedgeable, strict-liability claim held by every buyer, for the life of the offering, on the strength of an agency's current disposition not to pursue something it no longer controls.

That may still be worth doing. It is a real business decision and I am not telling anyone not to make it. But make it knowing that is the trade, rather than believing the question has been settled. Because the position founders are actually being offered is a hope.

The regulators influenced the law. Wishing they hadn't is not a risk strategy.
